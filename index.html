A Land of Makers

Sindh is home to one of the oldest civilizations in the world. More than four thousand years ago, the people of Mohenjo-daro were already shaping clay, weaving cloth and working with hides. Those skills passed from mother to daughter and father to son, village to village, until today.

Now machines, cheap imitations and changing lifestyles are pushing these crafts to the edge. Many artisans are old, and few young people are learning. This project shares the images and stories of three crafts so they are not lost.

01

Pottery

Kumbhar · The Potter's Art

Pottery is among the oldest arts of Sindh. Painted and wheel-made vessels were found at Mohenjo-daro and other Indus Valley sites, dating back over 4,500 years.

In later centuries, Sindh became known for glazed blue pottery and tile work, seen in places such as Hala and in the tiles that decorate the monuments of Thatta. Cobalt blue and turquoise glazes on white clay are its signature.

Why it is fading: Plastic and steel have replaced clay pots in most homes, and fewer families keep the potter's wheel turning.

02

Embroidery & Mirror Work

Sui Dhaga · Bharat · Abhla

Sindhi embroidery is a language told in thread. Women stitch dense geometric and floral patterns on dresses, cholis, cushions, camel decorations and wedding dowry pieces. Small round mirrors, called abhla, are stitched in to catch the light and, it is believed, to ward off the evil eye.

Each community, from Tharparkar to the Indus plains, has its own stitches, colors and motifs. A single piece can take weeks or even months of patient work.

Why it is fading: Machine embroidery is faster and cheaper, and young women often have little time or encouragement to learn the hand stitches.

03

Leather Work

Chamra · Khussa · Hand-stitched Hide

Leather craft has served Sindh's farmers, herders and travelers for generations. Artisans cure and cut hides by hand to make embroidered footwear, belts, bags, saddlery and camel gear for desert journeys.

Fine pieces are decorated with hand stitching, colored thread and small cut patterns. Each pair of shoes is shaped and sewn by hand, not by a machine line.

Why it is fading: Factory-made shoes and synthetic leather are cheaper, and the craft is often seen as hard work with little reward.

More Arts of Sindh

Many other crafts of this land are also growing quiet

Ajrak

Block-printed shawl

A shawl printed with carved wooden stamps in deep indigo and madder red. Natural and mineral dyes are used, and the printing is believed to go back to Indus Valley times. Ajrak is gifted as a mark of honor in Sindh.

Ralli Quilt

Appliqué & patchwork

Women join many layers of cloth with simple running stitches into bright geometric quilts. The name comes from the Sindhi word for "to mix and connect". Rallis are also given to daughters as dowry.

Bandhani

Tie-dye

Tiny knots are tied in cloth before dyeing, leaving patterns of dots. It is believed to have started in Sindh, and the Lar region and Thar desert are famous for it.

Hand Weaving

Lungi, Sussi, Khes & Khaddi

Sindh is known for silk and cotton lungis with gold-thread borders, striped sussi cloth from Hala, Hyderabad and Thatta, and warm khaddi shawls. Sussi was even exported to England in the 18th century.

Lacquer Work

Jandi jo kam

Wood from trees along the Indus is turned and coated with colored lacquer, then painted and varnished. It is used for the traditional swing (pingho), beds, chairs and lamps.

Wood Carving

Shikarpur

Carvers in Shikarpur cut detailed patterns into doors, windows and beds. Each carved panel is made by hand, with tools passed down in families.

Palm Leaf Basketry

Khairpur & Sukkur

Women weave baskets, mats, hand fans and bread plates from date palm leaves. They are often decorated with colorful pom-poms, metallic thread and small mirrors.

Camel Barber Art

Desert Sindh

Barbers clip a camel's hair into flowers and geometric patterns, sometimes adding henna or color. It is mostly done in the desert regions and is seen around festivals such as Eid.

Jewelry & Bangles

Silver, gold, glass

Jewelry making in Sindh goes back to the Indus Valley. Necklaces, nose rings, anklets and headpieces are made in silver and gold, and Hyderabad is famous for its glass bangles.

Beadwork & Sagi

Motin jo kam

Beads, shells, mirrors and pom-poms are sewn into hair braids (sagi), bags, drawstrings and decorations for buses and rickshaws.

Shell & Ivory Work

Almost vanished

The ancient port of Banbhore had a very large ivory workshop. Ivory carving has almost died out, but shell bangles and shell products are still made in lower Sindh.

Copper Bells

Metal craft

Hand-made copper bells for cattle and other animals ring across rural Sindh. Larger bells can also be seen hanging at the shrines of saints.
